## Junior Level (0–2 yrs)

### 1. What is Agile, at its core, and what problem was it a reaction to?
Agile is an iterative approach to software development emphasizing frequent delivery of working software, close collaboration with stakeholders, and adapting to changing requirements — codified in the 2001 Agile Manifesto's values (individuals/interactions over processes/tools, working software over comprehensive documentation, customer collaboration over contract negotiation, responding to change over following a plan). It was a reaction to "Waterfall" development, where requirements were fixed upfront and software wasn't delivered/validated with real users until very late in a long, rigid, sequential process — often discovering the requirements were wrong only after most of the effort was already spent.

### 2. What is Scrum, and what are its core ceremonies/events?
Scrum is a specific, structured framework for implementing Agile principles, organized around fixed-length iterations called **Sprints** (commonly 1-4 weeks). Core ceremonies: **Sprint Planning** (deciding what work to pull into the sprint), the **Daily Standup/Scrum** (a short daily sync on progress/blockers), the **Sprint Review** (demonstrating completed work to stakeholders), and the **Sprint Retrospective** (the team reflecting on how the sprint went and what to improve process-wise) — with a **Product Backlog** (prioritized list of all work) and **Sprint Backlog** (work committed to the current sprint) as the core artifacts.

### 3. What is Kanban, and how is it different from Scrum?
Kanban is a visual workflow-management method centered on a board with columns representing workflow stages (e.g. "To Do," "In Progress," "Done"), with **Work In Progress (WIP) limits** capping how many items can be in a given stage at once, encouraging finishing work before starting new work. Unlike Scrum, Kanban has no fixed-length sprints or prescribed ceremonies — work flows continuously and is pulled as capacity allows, making it a common fit for support/operations/DevOps work where incoming work (incidents, tickets) arrives unpredictably rather than being plannable into neat two-week increments.

### 4. What is a user story, and what does "INVEST" (a common quality checklist for user stories) stand for?
A user story is a short, plain-language description of a feature from the end user's perspective (commonly "As a [role], I want [goal], so that [benefit]"). **INVEST** — Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable — is a checklist for whether a story is well-formed: independent of other stories (can be worked/delivered without artificial ordering dependencies), negotiable (a discussion starting point, not an immutable spec), valuable to a real user/stakeholder, estimable (the team has enough clarity to size it), small (fits within a sprint), and testable (there's a clear way to verify it's actually done).

### 5. What does "Definition of Done" mean, and why does a team need one?
Definition of Done is an explicit, team-agreed checklist of what "complete" actually means for any piece of work (code reviewed, tests passing, deployed to staging, documentation updated) — without one, "done" becomes ambiguous and inconsistently interpreted (one engineer's "done" might mean code merged, another's might mean fully deployed and verified), leading to work that's reported complete but actually isn't ready, surfacing as unpleasant surprises later.

### 6. What is a retrospective, and why is a blameless approach to it valuable (see also the Incident Response file for the same principle applied to incidents)?
A retrospective is a recurring team meeting reflecting on a recent period of work (a sprint, or after a significant event) to identify what went well, what didn't, and concrete process improvements to try next. A blameless approach — focusing on systemic/process factors rather than blaming individuals — matters because a team that fears blame in retrospectives will hide problems and give sanitized, unhelpful feedback, defeating the entire purpose of the exercise, which is to surface real friction so it can actually be fixed.

### 7. What is the "DevOps" philosophy, in a sentence, and how does it relate to Agile?
DevOps extends Agile's iterative, collaborative philosophy past the boundary of "development is done" to include operations, deployment, and reliability — breaking down the traditional wall between development teams (who build software) and operations teams (who ran it, often with conflicting incentives: dev wants to ship fast, ops wants stability) so the same team/culture is responsible for both building *and* reliably operating what they build, with fast, automated feedback loops connecting the two.

### 8. What are the "Three Ways" of DevOps (from *The Phoenix Project*/DevOps Handbook)?
**The First Way** (Flow): optimizing the end-to-end flow of work from development to production, making work visible and removing bottlenecks. **The Second Way** (Feedback): creating fast, continuous feedback loops from right to left (production monitoring informing development, not just one-way delivery) so problems are caught and fixed quickly. **The Third Way** (Continual Learning/Experimentation): fostering a culture of continuous experimentation and learning from both successes and failures (blameless postmortems, dedicated time for improvement work) rather than a static, unchanging process.

### 9. What's the difference between a Product Owner and a Scrum Master, and what does each actually do day to day?
The **Product Owner** owns the *what* — prioritizing the product backlog based on business/user value, defining acceptance criteria, and being the primary point of contact for stakeholder questions/tradeoffs. The **Scrum Master** owns the *how the team works* — facilitating ceremonies, removing blockers/impediments for the team, and coaching the team on Agile/Scrum practice — a Scrum Master isn't a traditional manager assigning work, but a facilitator/servant-leader role focused on team process health.

### 10. What is a "sprint burndown chart," and what does it actually show?
A burndown chart plots remaining work (in story points or hours) against time within a sprint — an ideal, straight diagonal line represents steady, even progress toward zero remaining work by the sprint's end. A chart that flattens out (work isn't decreasing) signals the team is blocked or overcommitted; one that drops in sudden cliffs rather than a smooth line often signals work wasn't broken down into small enough pieces to show incremental progress.

## Mid Level (2–5 yrs)

### 11. What is the CALMS framework for evaluating DevOps maturity?
**Culture** (collaboration, shared ownership, blameless learning), **Automation** (automating repetitive manual work — builds, tests, deployments, infrastructure provisioning), **Lean** (small batch sizes, eliminating waste, fast feedback, similar to Lean manufacturing principles applied to software), **Measurement** (using real data — DORA metrics, SLOs — to drive decisions rather than intuition), and **Sharing** (knowledge/tooling sharing across teams rather than siloed practices) — a framework used to assess how far an organization has genuinely progressed in DevOps adoption across five distinct dimensions, rather than treating "DevOps" as synonymous with just adopting specific tools.

### 12. How would you actually apply Agile/Scrum practices to infrastructure/platform/DevOps work, given that on-call/incident response and unplanned operational work don't fit neatly into a two-week sprint plan?
Many infra/platform teams adapt rather than adopt Scrum wholesale — a common pattern is Kanban or a Scrum/Kanban hybrid ("Scrumban"), reserving explicit capacity/WIP-limited "slots" in each sprint (or continuously in a Kanban flow) for unplanned/reactive work (on-call interrupts, urgent bug fixes) alongside planned project work, rather than pretending 100% of a sprint's capacity is available for planned work and then being perpetually "behind" whenever unplanned operational work inevitably intrudes. Explicitly tracking and making visible the *proportion* of team capacity actually consumed by unplanned/toil work (rather than only tracking planned-work velocity) is itself valuable data — a team consistently spending 60% of its capacity on unplanned interrupts has a structural reliability/automation problem worth surfacing and addressing directly, which purely velocity-focused Scrum reporting can obscure if unplanned work isn't tracked at all.

### 13. What's the difference between story points and time-based estimation, and why do many Agile teams prefer story points despite them feeling more abstract?
Time-based estimation (hours/days) tries to predict absolute effort — notoriously unreliable because individual estimation skill/optimism varies wildly, and the same task can take very different absolute time for different people. **Story points** estimate *relative* complexity/effort/uncertainty (often using a Fibonacci-like scale: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13) compared to previously-estimated stories, deliberately abstracting away absolute time — a team calibrates its own "velocity" (points completed per sprint) empirically over multiple sprints, and that empirically-derived velocity (not the story points' face value) becomes the actual basis for forecasting, which tends to average out individual estimation bias better than asking people to directly predict hours, provided the team is reasonably consistent internally (points aren't meaningfully comparable *across* different teams, only within one team's own historical calibration).

### 14. What's the difference between "technical debt" and simply "code/infrastructure that needs improvement," and how do you make the case for prioritizing paying it down against competing feature work?
Not all imperfect code is technical debt in the meaningful sense — the term specifically describes a *deliberate or emergent shortcut* that trades faster short-term delivery for a longer-term cost (harder-to-maintain code, fragile infrastructure, missing tests) that compounds if left unaddressed, analogous to financial debt accruing interest. The strongest case for prioritizing debt paydown ties it to a concrete, currently-felt cost, not an abstract "clean code" argument — quantifying how a specific piece of debt is *actively* slowing down current feature delivery (every change to this module takes 3x longer due to lacking tests), increasing incident frequency/severity (this fragile deployment process caused two of our last three outages), or blocking a near-term roadmap item outright — framing it in terms stakeholders outside engineering can actually weigh against other priorities, rather than expecting technical debt paydown to be prioritized purely on engineering aesthetic grounds.

### 15. What is Conway's Law, and why does it matter when designing team structure alongside system/microservices architecture?
Conway's Law observes that organizations design systems that mirror their own communication structure — in practice, a system's architecture tends to end up shaped by which teams talk to (or don't talk to) each other, regardless of the architecture diagram's original intent. This matters directly for DevOps/platform teams designing a microservices architecture: if you want genuinely independent, loosely-coupled services, the *teams* owning them generally need to be similarly independent (clear ownership boundaries, minimal cross-team coordination required for a typical change) — attempting a decoupled microservices architecture while keeping tightly-coupled, constantly-cross-communicating team structures (the "reverse Conway maneuver" is deliberately restructuring teams *first* to get the system architecture you actually want) tends to produce services that are technically separate but operationally entangled anyway.

## Senior Level (5+ yrs)

### 16. You've joined an organization where "Agile" has become a purely ceremonial process — daily standups and sprint planning happen, but delivery is still slow and unpredictable. How do you diagnose what's actually wrong, and what would you change?
Distinguish between the *ceremonies* of Agile (which can be performed without delivering any of Agile's actual benefit) and the underlying outcomes Agile is meant to produce (fast feedback, small batch sizes, genuine adaptability to change, sustainable pace) — a team can run every Scrum ceremony flawlessly while still working in large, infrequently-integrated batches, having stories that are consistently far too large to fit meaningfully within a sprint, or having a Product Owner who isn't actually empowered to make real prioritization decisions (every priority call escalates elsewhere, defeating fast local decision-making). Diagnose with real data rather than assumption: look at actual cycle time (how long does work sit from "started" to "actually delivered," not just sprint-to-sprint velocity, which can look fine even while cycle time for individual items is terrible), how often stories carry over incomplete between sprints (a strong signal of poor breakdown/estimation), and how much of the team's stated capacity is actually consumed by unplanned/interrupt work versus what's assumed in planning. The fix is rarely "run the ceremonies harder" — it's usually addressing the underlying structural issue the ceremonies were papering over: genuinely smaller, better-defined stories; a Product Owner with real decision authority; addressing a chronic imbalance between planned and unplanned work explicitly rather than silently absorbing it; or, if cross-team dependencies are the actual bottleneck, addressing team/ownership boundaries (Conway's Law) rather than assuming a process tweak within one team can fix a structural cross-team problem.

### 17. How would you drive genuine DevOps cultural change in an organization with an entrenched, siloed Dev-vs-Ops divide (separate teams, separate incentives, a history of blaming each other during incidents)?
Cultural change of this kind rarely succeeds as a top-down mandate or a rebranding exercise (renaming the Ops team "DevOps" without changing anything structural is a common, ineffective anti-pattern) — durable change usually starts with a concrete, visible win that demonstrates the value directly to skeptics, rather than an abstract pitch about "DevOps culture": picking a specific, painful, currently-manual process (a deployment that takes hours and requires an ops engineer paged in) and jointly automating/improving it with both dev and ops engineers working together, so the improved outcome (faster, safer, less painful deploys) is directly experienced by people who were skeptical, not just described to them. Structurally, shifting incentives matters more than any single initiative — as long as a "dev" team is measured purely on feature velocity and an "ops" team is measured purely on uptime/stability with no shared accountability for either, their interests will keep pulling in opposite directions regardless of cultural messaging; shared metrics/on-call ownership (developers carrying pager duty for what they ship, not just an ops team) directly aligns incentives in a way that talking about "shared ownership" alone doesn't. Blameless postmortems, applied consistently and genuinely (not performatively, with real senior leadership buy-in when a postmortem's honest findings are uncomfortable), are what actually make people willing to be honest about failure across the old Dev/Ops boundary rather than reflexively protecting their own team's reputation during an incident review — this is slow, sustained cultural work measured in the consistency of how leadership responds to the first few genuinely hard postmortems, not a single training session or policy announcement.

### 18. How do you balance standardizing engineering process/tooling across many teams (for consistency, onboarding ease, and platform investment) against the real risk of over-centralizing and slowing every team down to the pace of the most cautious/bureaucratic common denominator?
The productive framing is distinguishing what genuinely benefits from standardization (shared, non-differentiating infrastructure/tooling — CI/CD platform, deployment mechanics, observability stack, security baseline — where every team reinventing their own version is pure duplicated effort with no offsetting benefit) from what should stay genuinely team-autonomous (how a specific team runs its own internal process/ceremonies, technical decisions specific to their own service's domain problems) — over-centralization typically happens when this distinction isn't made explicitly, and *process* (how teams plan/estimate/retro) gets centrally mandated alongside genuinely shared *infrastructure*, even though team-specific process autonomy rarely has the same duplication cost that reinventing shared infrastructure does. Practically, treat centrally-provided platform/tooling as an internal product that has to *earn* adoption through being genuinely better/faster than a team building it themselves (per the golden-path framing covered in the Deployment/System Design file) rather than only through mandate — a mandate without the tooling actually being good enough will produce compliance theater or covert workarounds, while investing in the tooling being genuinely excellent produces voluntary adoption that scales far more sustainably, and reserves whatever *mandatory* standardization actually exists for the narrow set of things with genuine cross-team risk if left inconsistent (security baselines, compliance-relevant controls) rather than applying uniform mandate-level rigidity to every process decision a team makes.
