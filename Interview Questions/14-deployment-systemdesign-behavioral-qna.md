## Junior Level (0–2 yrs)

### 1. What is a deployment strategy, and why not just always "stop the old version, start the new one"?
A deployment strategy is the approach used to roll out a new application version safely. The naive "stop old, start new" approach (recreate deployment) causes a period of complete downtime between stopping the old version and the new one becoming ready — acceptable for some internal/low-criticality tools, but unacceptable for most user-facing production services, which is why more sophisticated strategies (rolling, blue-green, canary) exist specifically to avoid or minimize that downtime window.

### 2. What is a rolling deployment?
A rolling deployment gradually replaces old-version instances with new-version instances a few at a time (rather than all at once), keeping the service available throughout — at any given moment during the rollout, some instances run the old version and some run the new version simultaneously, which requires the two versions to be at least compatible enough to coexist briefly (particularly regarding any shared database schema or API contract).

### 3. What is a blue-green deployment?
Blue-green deployment maintains two identical, complete environments — "blue" (currently live) and "green" (the new version, deployed but not yet receiving traffic). Once the green environment is deployed and validated, traffic is switched over to it, typically at a router/load-balancer level, essentially all at once. If a problem is found, traffic can be switched back to blue almost instantly, making rollback very fast — the cost is needing double the infrastructure, at least during the transition.

### 4. What is a canary deployment?
A canary deployment routes a small percentage of real production traffic to the new version first (the "canary"), while most traffic continues to the stable version — if the canary shows healthy metrics (error rate, latency) over an observation period, traffic is gradually increased until the new version fully replaces the old one; if problems appear, only that small percentage of traffic was ever affected, and the canary can be rolled back quickly.

### 5. What's the difference between a deployment strategy and a feature flag?
A deployment strategy controls *how a new build/version of code* is rolled out at the infrastructure level. A **feature flag** controls whether a specific *feature within already-deployed code* is active, toggled independently of any actual deployment — letting you deploy code with a new feature dark/disabled, then enable it for specific users/percentages later (or instantly disable it if it causes problems) without needing a new deployment at all. The two are complementary — feature flags let you decouple "deploying code" from "releasing a feature" entirely.

### 6. What is downtime, and what's the difference between planned and unplanned downtime?
Downtime is any period a service is unavailable or degraded for users. Planned downtime is scheduled and communicated in advance (a maintenance window) — still disruptive, but expected and controllable. Unplanned downtime results from an unexpected failure/incident — the primary thing SRE/reliability practices and good deployment strategies aim to minimize, since it directly and unpredictably impacts users and trust.

### 7. What is a "system design" question in a DevOps interview, and how is it different from a coding interview's system design question?
A DevOps/infrastructure system design question asks you to design the *infrastructure and operational architecture* around a system (e.g. "design a scalable, reliable CI/CD pipeline," "design a monitoring solution for a fleet of 1,000 servers") rather than a software engineering system design question focused on application-level architecture (e.g. designing a URL shortener's data model and API). Interviewers are typically evaluating how you reason about tradeoffs, scalability, reliability, and cost — not looking for one single "correct" diagram.

### 8. Why do interviewers ask behavioral questions ("tell me about a time you...") in a technical DevOps interview?
Because DevOps/SRE roles involve significant collaboration, incident response under pressure, and judgment calls that pure technical knowledge doesn't reveal — behavioral questions probe how you actually behaved in real past situations (communication during an incident, how you handled disagreement with a teammate, how you prioritized competing demands) as a predictor of how you'll behave in similar future situations, which technical trivia questions can't assess.

### 9. What is the STAR method for answering behavioral interview questions?
**Situation** (brief context), **Task** (what needed to be done/your responsibility), **Action** (what you specifically did — the bulk of the answer), **Result** (the outcome, ideally with a concrete measure or clear impact, plus what you learned). It keeps behavioral answers concrete and complete rather than vague or overly abstract, and specifically emphasizes "what *you* did" even when describing team efforts, since interviewers are trying to assess your individual contribution and judgment.

### 10. What's a reasonable way to answer "why do you want to work here / why DevOps" without sounding generic?
Ground the answer in something specific and genuine — a particular technical challenge the company's scale/domain presents that interests you, something concrete you learned about their engineering culture/practices from research (blog posts, their open-source work, specific tech choices), or a specific aspect of DevOps work itself (the intersection of building and operating systems, the direct feedback loop between your automation work and real reliability outcomes) that genuinely motivates you — generic answers ("I like the mission," "great culture") signal a lack of real research/reflection, while specific ones signal genuine interest and preparation.

## Mid Level (2–5 yrs)

### 11. What is a shadow deployment (dark launch/traffic shadowing/mirroring), and how is it different from a canary?
Shadow deployment duplicates real production traffic and sends a copy to the new version *in addition to* the normal request still being served by the current stable version — the new version's responses are discarded/never actually shown to real users, used purely to validate its behavior (does it error, how does its latency compare, does it produce the same output) against real traffic patterns with **zero user-facing risk**, since real users never actually see the shadowed version's response. This differs fundamentally from a canary, where the canary version's response *is* actually served to the (small percentage of) real users it's routed to, carrying genuine (if limited) user-facing risk — shadowing is strictly safer but can't validate certain things a canary can (actual user behavior/conversion impact, since shadowed responses are never truly experienced by users).

### 12. What's the difference between an A/B test and a canary deployment, given both involve splitting traffic between two versions?
A canary deployment's *purpose* is safely validating technical health (errors, latency, crashes) of a new release before full rollout — once validated, the canary version becomes the sole, permanent version and the comparison ends. An **A/B test**'s purpose is measuring the actual *business/product impact* of a deliberate difference between two versions (which converts better, which drives more engagement) — often run for a statistically meaningful duration with both variants intentionally continuing to coexist (not marching toward one fully replacing the other), and evaluated on product/business metrics rather than primarily technical health metrics. The traffic-splitting *mechanism* underneath can look similar or even be shared infrastructure, but the goal and how you interpret/act on the results differ substantially.

### 13. What are common deployment anti-patterns, and why are they problematic?
Deploying directly to production without first validating in a staging/pre-production environment (removes your safety net for catching integration issues before real users see them); deploying large, infrequent "big bang" releases bundling many changes together (when something breaks, it's much harder to isolate *which* of the many bundled changes caused it, compared to small, frequent releases); lacking an automated or well-rehearsed rollback path (discovering during an actual incident that rollback is manual, slow, or has never actually been tested is a common and entirely avoidable source of extended outage duration); and deploying without adequate automated testing/health-check gating between stages (relying on a human noticing a problem rather than automated checks catching it immediately) — each represents a way that convenience/speed shortcuts in the deployment process directly translate into higher risk and longer incident duration when something does go wrong.

### 14. Design a CI/CD and deployment pipeline for a startup's first production service — what would you prioritize given limited engineering time, versus what can reasonably wait?
Prioritize first: automated tests running in CI on every PR (catches regressions before merge, the highest-leverage investment for a small team), a straightforward automated deployment (even a simple rolling deployment via a managed platform is far better than manual deploys, which don't scale past a couple of engineers and are highly error-prone under time pressure), and basic health checks/monitoring with alerting on the handful of signals that would actually indicate the service is down or badly broken (don't over-invest in exhaustive observability before you even have meaningful production traffic to observe). Reasonably deferred until the team/traffic actually justifies the complexity: sophisticated canary/blue-green deployment infrastructure (a simple rolling deployment with good tests and fast rollback covers most early-stage risk at a fraction of the setup cost), a full multi-region/DR architecture (premature for most early-stage products relative to their actual current business risk), and elaborate internal tooling/platform investment (a small team's engineering time is usually far better spent on the product itself early on) — the general principle for a resource-constrained team is matching operational sophistication to actual current risk/scale, not building for a scale/reliability bar you don't have yet at the expense of never reaching meaningful scale at all.

### 15. (System design) Design a CI/CD pipeline and deployment strategy for a payments-processing service where correctness and uptime are both critical. Walk through your reasoning.
Given the stakes, favor safety over speed at every decision point relative to a typical service: extensive automated testing including contract/integration tests against dependent services (not just unit tests), a staging environment closely mirroring production (including realistic data volume/shape, since payment logic edge cases often only surface at realistic scale/data variety), and a canary deployment strategy specifically (not a plain rolling deployment) so a bad release is caught on a small, bounded slice of real transactions rather than potentially affecting all transactions simultaneously — with automated rollback triggered by concrete health signals (transaction failure rate, latency, reconciliation mismatches) rather than requiring a human to notice and manually intervene, since even a few minutes of a broken payment path can have significant financial and trust impact. Idempotency in the payment processing logic itself (so a retried request, whether from a client retry or from failover/rollback recovery, never double-charges) is a design requirement threading through this whole pipeline, not just an infrastructure concern — and given the correctness stakes, a slower, more heavily gated deployment cadence for this specific service (compared to a less critical internal tool) is a reasonable, deliberate tradeoff of velocity for safety, worth explicitly justifying rather than apologizing for in an interview answer.

### 16. (Behavioral) "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision made by a teammate or your manager. What did you do?"
A strong answer (using STAR) describes a specific real disagreement, explains the reasoning behind your position (showing sound technical judgment, not just stubbornness), describes how you raised it constructively (data/reasoning-based, not just asserting you were right, and genuinely listening to the other perspective rather than only advocating for your own) and — importantly — describes the actual outcome honestly, including if you ended up implementing an approach you disagreed with once a decision was made (showing you can disagree constructively and then commit, a trait interviewers specifically value, since teams that can't do this either become paralyzed by unresolved disagreement or fracture into people quietly working around decisions they didn't get their way on) versus outcomes where your pushback did change the direction (showing your reasoning was sound and persuasive) — either honest outcome is a fine answer; what interviewers are actually screening for is the *process* (constructive disagreement, evidence-based reasoning, ability to commit once a decision is made) more than which specific outcome occurred.

### 17. (Behavioral) "Tell me about a production incident you were involved in. What was your role, and what did you learn?"
A strong answer clearly states your specific role and actions (not just "the team fixed it" — what did *you* personally investigate/decide/do), describes the actual diagnostic/mitigation process concretely enough to demonstrate genuine technical depth (not a vague summary), and — critically — describes concrete follow-up/learning (a specific system improvement, a runbook you wrote, a monitoring gap you closed) rather than ending the story at "and then it was fixed," since interviewers are specifically listening for whether you treat incidents as learning/improvement opportunities (the blameless-postmortem mindset) rather than just isolated firefighting events with no lasting change afterward.

## Senior Level (5+ yrs)

### 18. (System design) Design the infrastructure and deployment architecture for a globally-distributed, latency-sensitive real-time application (e.g. a multiplayer game backend or a real-time collaboration tool) serving users across multiple continents. Walk through your key decisions and tradeoffs.
Key decisions to reason through explicitly, not just name: **regional placement** — deploy compute close to users across multiple regions (reducing round-trip latency, which for real-time applications directly determines perceived quality far more than for typical request/response web traffic) using anycast or geo-routing (GeoDNS, or a global L7 load balancer with latency-based routing) to route each user to their nearest healthy region. **State/data consistency strategy** — real-time collaborative or multiplayer state typically can't tolerate the latency of cross-region synchronous consistency for every interaction, so the design likely needs to reason explicitly about which state must be strongly consistent (account/billing data — fine to centralize, latency there is much less user-visible) versus state that can be regionally-local/eventually-consistent (in-session real-time state, often authoritative within one region for the duration of a session, with users assigned to a specific regional "room"/session server rather than state being globally synchronized per-interaction). **Deployment strategy implications** — a canary/gradual rollout strategy needs to account for *per-region* rollout sequencing (rolling a change out region-by-region, validating each before proceeding, rather than globally simultaneously) given that a bug might only manifest under one region's specific traffic pattern/load characteristics. **Failure handling** — given the latency sensitivity, failover to a further-away healthy region is a real, user-noticeable degradation (not free/transparent the way it might be for a batch/less-latency-sensitive workload), so the design should reason explicitly about what degraded-but-functional behavior looks like during a regional failure, not just "does it eventually recover." A strong answer explicitly surfaces and reasons about these tradeoffs rather than presenting a single architecture as if it were the only correct answer — system design interviews are evaluating the reasoning process as much as the final diagram.

### 19. (System design) You're asked to design a platform-engineering "golden path" for application teams to self-service deploy new services, balancing standardization against team autonomy. What would you build, and how do you get teams to actually adopt it rather than routing around it?
Build a small number of well-designed, opinionated templates/scaffolding (a service template generating a working CI/CD pipeline, base Dockerfile/Kubernetes manifests, standard observability instrumentation, and security scanning already wired in) that get a new service from zero to a working, production-ready deployment in a genuinely short amount of time — the adoption lever is that the golden path needs to be **meaningfully easier and faster** than a team building their own equivalent from scratch, not merely "the approved/mandated way," since a platform team can mandate a path but can't force genuine engagement, and a path that's slower or more restrictive than teams doing it themselves reliably gets quietly routed around (shadow infrastructure, teams maintaining their own parallel pipeline) regardless of the mandate. Preserve genuine team autonomy at the *application logic* layer (what the service does, its own business logic, its own service-specific configuration) while standardizing the *platform/operational* layer (how it's built, deployed, secured, observed) — and treat the platform as an internal product with real user feedback loops (office hours, an internal Slack channel, iterating on the templates based on real friction teams report) rather than a static mandate handed down once and never revisited, since golden paths that don't evolve with real usage friction fossilize into exactly the kind of restrictive, resented tooling that drives the shadow-infrastructure outcome you were trying to avoid in the first place.

### 20. (Behavioral) "Tell me about a time you had to make a difficult tradeoff between shipping quickly and doing things 'the right way' (technical debt, cutting corners on testing/reliability)." How do you evaluate a strong answer to this as an interviewer, and how should you structure it as a candidate?
A strong answer as a candidate goes beyond "we shipped fast and it worked out" (survivorship, not judgment) or "we always insist on doing it right" (unrealistic, and signals inflexibility to real business constraints) — it demonstrates an explicit, reasoned tradeoff: what specifically was cut and why that specific thing was judged lower-risk to defer (not everything is equally risky to cut), what compensating controls were put in place given the shortcut (extra monitoring on the specific area you knew was riskier, a concrete plan/ticket to actually revisit the debt rather than a vague intention that never happens), and ideally the follow-through (did the team actually come back and address it, and if not, why not, and what did you learn about how technical debt actually gets prioritized or deprioritized in practice at your organization). As an interviewer evaluating this answer, the signal to look for isn't "did they choose speed or quality" in isolation — it's whether the candidate can articulate the *reasoning* behind a tradeoff decision and shows awareness of its consequences and follow-through, since that judgment (not a categorical rule in either direction) is what a senior engineer is actually trusted to exercise repeatedly on real, ambiguous production tradeoffs.

### 21. (Behavioral / leadership) "How do you mentor junior engineers or influence technical direction without formal authority over a team?"
A strong senior-level answer moves past generic "I pair program and do code reviews" into specifics that demonstrate actual influence-without-authority skill: building credibility through consistently sound technical judgment and follow-through (influence is earned through a track record, not claimed), teaching *reasoning* rather than just handing over answers (walking through *why* an approach is better in a code review comment, not just "change this to that," so the junior engineer's judgment actually improves over time rather than only that one instance of code), and — for cross-team technical influence without direct authority — building consensus through clear written proposals/RFCs with explicit tradeoffs laid out and actively seeking dissenting feedback before trying to drive a decision, rather than relying on seniority/title alone to make a technical direction land. A genuinely strong answer includes a specific example where this actually worked (a concrete technical direction you influenced, or a specific engineer whose growth you can point to concretely) rather than staying entirely abstract/generic, since abstract answers to this question are common and don't differentiate a candidate the way a specific, honest example does.

### 22. (Behavioral) "Describe a time a project or initiative you led failed or fell significantly short of its goal. What did you do, and what would you do differently?"
This question specifically screens for self-awareness and genuine learning from failure, as distinct from technical competence (which other questions already cover) — a weak answer either deflects blame entirely onto external factors/other people, or picks a "failure" that isn't a real, meaningful one (a humble-brag disguised as a failure story), both of which read as a lack of genuine self-reflection. A strong answer honestly owns the parts of the failure that were within your control/judgment (a scope you underestimated, a stakeholder you failed to bring along early enough, a technical approach that turned out to be wrong and the earlier signal you missed that should have prompted a course-correction sooner), articulates specifically what you'd do differently with the benefit of that hindsight (not just "I'd work harder" — a specific, different decision or process change), and — ideally — describes how you've actually applied that lesson in a subsequent situation, demonstrating the learning was real and lasting rather than only reflective in the interview itself.
