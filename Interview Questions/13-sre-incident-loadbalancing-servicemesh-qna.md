## Junior Level (0–2 yrs)

### 1. What is Site Reliability Engineering (SRE), and how is it different from traditional Ops/sysadmin?
SRE (originated at Google) applies software engineering approaches to operations problems — treating reliability as something you measure quantitatively (SLIs/SLOs/error budgets) and improve through automation and engineering, rather than through manual, ad hoc firefighting. A common summary: "SRE is what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations function" — with an emphasis on automating away repetitive manual work ("toil") rather than accepting it as a permanent cost of running production systems.

### 2. What is an incident, and why does severity/priority classification matter?
An incident is an unplanned event that degrades or interrupts a service. Classifying severity (e.g. SEV1 = full outage/critical impact, SEV3 = minor, low-impact issue) matters because it determines response urgency, who gets paged, and what communication/escalation process kicks in — treating every issue with the same urgency either burns out responders on non-urgent issues or, worse, under-responds to genuinely critical ones by not distinguishing them from routine noise.

### 3. What is on-call, and what's a reasonable on-call rotation practice?
On-call means a designated engineer is responsible for responding to alerts/incidents outside normal working hours for a defined period (a "shift"). Reasonable practice includes rotating the responsibility fairly across a team (not the same 1-2 people every time), keeping shift lengths sustainable (commonly a week), having a clear escalation path if the primary on-call doesn't acknowledge an alert within a defined window, and compensating on-call time appropriately (pay, time off) since it constrains someone's personal time even when no incident actually occurs.

### 4. What is a postmortem, and what does "blameless" mean in that context?
A postmortem (or "retrospective") is a structured written account of an incident — what happened, its impact, the timeline, root cause(s), and action items to prevent recurrence. "Blameless" means the process focuses on *systemic* factors that allowed the incident to happen (a missing safeguard, insufficient testing, unclear runbook) rather than blaming the individual(s) involved — because a culture that punishes people for incidents/mistakes reliably makes people hide or downplay problems in the future, which makes the *organization* less reliable overall, even if it feels like accountability in the moment.

### 5. What is a load balancer's health check, and why does it need to exist separately from a Kubernetes liveness/readiness probe?
A load balancer's health check periodically verifies a backend target is actually able to serve traffic, removing unhealthy targets from rotation. In a Kubernetes context, the load balancer's own health check (often just checking the Service/target group) and Kubernetes' readiness probe serve complementary purposes — readiness probes control whether a Pod receives traffic *from the Kubernetes Service* at all, while an external/cloud load balancer's health check operates one layer further out and may have different criteria/timing, so both need to be configured coherently (or you risk a Pod being marked "ready" by Kubernetes while the external LB still (or never) considers it healthy, or vice versa).

### 6. What are the basic load balancing algorithms — round robin, least connections, and IP hash?
**Round robin** distributes requests to backends in simple rotating order, regardless of each backend's current load — simple, but can send traffic to an already-overloaded backend if request costs vary significantly. **Least connections** routes each new request to whichever backend currently has the fewest active connections — better balances load when request processing time varies. **IP hash** consistently routes requests from the same client IP to the same backend — provides basic session affinity/"stickiness" without needing application-level session sharing, at the cost of uneven distribution if traffic isn't evenly spread across client IPs.

### 7. What is a service mesh, at a basic level, and what problem does it solve?
A service mesh is a dedicated infrastructure layer (typically a per-Pod "sidecar" proxy, like Envoy in Istio) that handles service-to-service communication concerns — mTLS encryption, retries, timeouts, load balancing, and observability (metrics/tracing) — uniformly across all services, without requiring every individual application to implement that networking logic itself in every language/framework it happens to be written in.

### 8. What is chaos engineering, at a basic level?
Chaos engineering is the practice of deliberately, in a controlled way, injecting failures into a system (killing a service instance, adding network latency, exhausting a resource) to verify the system actually behaves resiliently the way you assume it does — rather than only discovering during a real, unplanned outage that an assumed failover/retry/redundancy mechanism doesn't actually work as designed.

### 9. What is a runbook, and why is having one valuable during an incident?
A runbook is a documented, step-by-step procedure for diagnosing or resolving a specific, known type of issue (e.g. "database connection pool exhaustion: check X, then do Y"). Having one valuable during an incident because it lets a responder (even one less familiar with that specific system) follow a tested procedure under time pressure, rather than improvising from scratch while stressed and while the clock on user impact keeps running — well-maintained runbooks are one of the most direct ways to reduce Mean Time To Resolution (MTTR).

### 10. What's the difference between MTTD, MTTA, and MTTR?
**MTTD** (Mean Time To Detect) is how long it takes from when a problem starts to when it's actually noticed/detected (by monitoring/alerting, ideally, rather than by a customer complaint). **MTTA** (Mean Time To Acknowledge) is how long from detection to a responder actually acknowledging/starting to work the incident. **MTTR** (Mean Time To Resolve/Recover) is how long from detection (or sometimes from acknowledgment, definitions vary) to full resolution. Together they decompose "how long did this incident actually hurt users" into distinct phases, each improvable by different interventions (better alerting reduces MTTD; better on-call/escalation reduces MTTA; better runbooks/automation/architecture reduces MTTR).

## Mid Level (2–5 yrs)

### 11. Explain the concept of "toil" in SRE, and why reducing it is treated as a first-class engineering priority rather than just "operations being operations."
Toil is manual, repetitive, tactical operational work that scales linearly with service growth and provides no long-term engineering value (a manual deployment step repeated every release, manually restarting a service that crashes in a known way, manually provisioning access requests). It's treated as a first-class priority to eliminate (via automation) because unchecked toil consumes an ever-growing share of an SRE/ops team's time as the systems they support grow, eventually leaving no capacity left for the actual engineering work (better architecture, better tooling, genuine reliability improvements) that would reduce future toil and incidents — Google's SRE practice famously caps the fraction of time spent on toil (historically targeting below 50%) specifically to prevent teams from being permanently consumed by an ever-growing operational burden instead of improving the systems that create it.

### 12. Walk through what a healthy incident command structure looks like during a major (SEV1) incident with multiple responders.
A single **Incident Commander (IC)** owns overall coordination and decision-making authority for the incident (not necessarily the most senior/technical person — the IC's job is coordination and communication, not personally debugging the issue), preventing the common failure mode of multiple people independently investigating/fixing without coordination (which risks duplicated effort or, worse, conflicting fixes made simultaneously). Distinct roles are typically separated: technical responders actively investigating/mitigating, a **communications lead** keeping stakeholders/status pages updated (freeing technical responders from interruption), and a **scribe** maintaining a timestamped log of actions/decisions in real time (invaluable for the postmortem afterward, and for keeping the IC's own mental model of "what have we already tried" accurate as an incident stretches on). This structure (borrowed from the incident command system used in emergency services) matters more as incident complexity/duration grows — a two-minute blip needs none of this ceremony, but a multi-hour, multi-team incident without it reliably degrades into confusion.

### 13. What's the difference between mitigating an incident and actually resolving its root cause, and why is prioritizing mitigation first usually correct?
Mitigation stops or reduces active user impact *right now* (rolling back a bad deploy, failing over to a healthy region, restarting a stuck process) without necessarily understanding or fixing the underlying root cause yet. Root cause resolution addresses *why* the problem happened, preventing recurrence, but often takes longer to properly diagnose and implement. Prioritizing mitigation first is usually correct because it stops the bleeding for actual users immediately, and root-cause analysis can then proceed carefully (often via a full postmortem after the fact) without users continuing to suffer degraded service throughout that slower diagnostic process — the exception is when a hasty mitigation risks making things meaningfully worse or destroying diagnostic evidence needed for root-cause analysis, which is a judgment call the incident commander needs to make explicitly rather than reflexively rolling back or restarting.

### 14. Explain the difference between a canary release with automated rollback and a chaos engineering "game day" — both involve deliberately testing under adverse conditions, but for what different purposes?
A canary release tests a *specific, real, upcoming change* against a small slice of real production traffic, with automated (or manual) rollback if it shows problems — the purpose is validating a specific deployment before full rollout, as part of normal, routine delivery. A chaos engineering game day deliberately injects *hypothetical* failure conditions (killing a dependency, simulating an AZ outage) into a system that isn't otherwise being changed, specifically to validate that *assumed* resilience mechanisms (failover, retries, circuit breakers) actually work as designed — the purpose is proactively discovering unknown weaknesses in steady-state architecture, independent of any specific code change, rather than validating one particular deployment.

### 15. What is a circuit breaker pattern, and how does it prevent cascading failures?
A circuit breaker monitors calls to a downstream dependency, and after a threshold of failures/timeouts, "opens" — immediately failing subsequent calls to that dependency (without even attempting them) for a cooldown period, rather than letting every caller keep waiting on/retrying an already-struggling dependency. This prevents a single failing/slow downstream service from exhausting the calling service's own resources (threads, connections) waiting on doomed calls, which is exactly the mechanism by which one failing service can cascade into taking down services that depend on it — after the cooldown, the circuit breaker periodically allows a limited "trial" request through to check if the dependency has recovered, closing again (resuming normal calls) if it has.

### 16. What's the difference between sticky sessions (session affinity) and a stateless application design, and why do most modern architectures prefer the latter?
Sticky sessions route all of a given client's requests to the *same* backend instance (via a cookie or IP hash), so that instance can keep session state in local memory. This creates real operational problems: uneven load distribution (a session-heavy client "sticks" to one backend regardless of that backend's current load), and it breaks or degrades gracefully-handling deploys/scaling (killing that specific instance loses its in-memory session state for every client stuck to it). Modern architectures prefer **stateless** application instances (any instance can serve any request) with session state externalized to a shared store (Redis, a database) — this allows any backend to serve any request, enabling clean horizontal scaling, rolling deployments, and load balancing without needing session affinity as a workaround.

### 17. What is Istio (or a service mesh generally) actually providing that you couldn't get from Kubernetes NetworkPolicies and a standard Ingress alone?
NetworkPolicies control coarse-grained *connectivity* (can Pod A reach Pod B at all) — they don't encrypt traffic, don't provide fine-grained per-request authorization, retries, or circuit breaking, and don't provide detailed per-service traffic metrics/tracing. A service mesh adds mTLS encryption and cryptographic service identity (rather than trusting network location alone), fine-grained L7 traffic policy (canary/traffic-splitting between service versions, retries/timeouts/circuit-breaking configured declaratively rather than hand-coded into every application), and uniform observability (automatic golden-signal metrics and tracing for every service-to-service call, without each service needing to implement this itself) — capabilities that sit above what NetworkPolicies and a basic Ingress controller provide, at the cost of the mesh's own operational complexity and resource overhead (a sidecar proxy per Pod).

### 18. Compare a rolling deployment, blue-green deployment, and canary deployment from a reliability/blast-radius perspective (see also the dedicated Deployment Strategies file for full depth).
A **rolling** deployment replaces instances incrementally with no separate full second environment — simple and resource-efficient, but a bad release is only caught after it's already serving some percentage of real traffic, and rollback means rolling forward again with the old version (not instant). **Blue-green** keeps two full environments and switches traffic atomically — very fast rollback (switch back), but requires double the infrastructure (at least temporarily) and doesn't inherently limit *how much* traffic hits the new version before you've validated it (the switch is typically all-or-nothing, unless combined with weighted traffic splitting). **Canary** exposes the new version to a small, controlled percentage of real traffic first, only increasing exposure as health signals confirm safety — the best blast-radius containment of the three, at the cost of the most operational complexity (traffic-splitting infrastructure, automated health-signal-based promotion/rollback logic) and the slowest full rollout.

## Senior Level (5+ yrs)

### 19. Design an on-call and incident management program for an organization scaling from a single team to many teams, addressing sustainability and avoiding burnout.
As team count grows, avoid a single, centralized on-call rotation covering every service (doesn't scale — the on-call engineer can't reasonably understand every system well enough to respond effectively, and burns out faster from sheer alert volume across unrelated systems) in favor of **service-ownership-aligned on-call** — each team owns on-call for the services they actually built and understand, with a clear, documented escalation path to adjacent teams/an incident commander for genuinely cross-cutting incidents. Enforce real sustainability guardrails, not just aspirational policy: cap alert volume per shift (an on-call rotation that pages someone 20 times a night, every week, is an alerting/architecture problem to fix, not a staffing problem to just add more rotation depth to paper over), ensure adequate rotation depth (enough people in a rotation that any individual's on-call frequency stays reasonable — very small teams with only 2-3 people in rotation is a real structural risk worth escalating, not something to just accept), and track/act on on-call load and postmortem action-item completion as an actual leadership-visible metric, since "we'll fix the noisy alert eventually" without any forcing function reliably never happens once the immediate pain of an incident fades. Culturally, blameless postmortems and psychological safety around raising "this on-call rotation is unsustainable" are prerequisites for any of the above structural fixes actually surfacing and getting prioritized rather than being silently endured.

### 20. Walk through architecting resilience for a critical service against a full regional cloud outage — what specifically needs to be true for automated failover to actually work when you need it, not just on paper?
Beyond simply "deploy to multiple regions," genuine regional failover requires: **data replication that's actually current enough** at failover time (synchronous cross-region replication guarantees zero data loss but adds latency to every write; asynchronous replication is faster but means failover accepts some data loss/staleness — an explicit, deliberate tradeoff that must match the actual business RPO requirement, not an accidental byproduct of whatever replication mode was easiest to set up); **failover automation that's actually tested regularly** (a documented manual failover runbook that's never been executed end-to-end is a common source of "the failover itself failed" during a real regional outage — game days that actually execute real failover, not just review the runbook, are what build genuine confidence); **capacity headroom in the failover region** to actually absorb full production load (a DR region sized only for its own normal baseline traffic, not the full failed-over load, silently fails exactly when needed most); and **dependencies that don't quietly reintroduce a single-region bottleneck** — a multi-region application that still depends on a single-region control-plane service, a single-region DNS/certificate management dependency, or a single-region secrets manager instance reintroduces exactly the single point of failure the multi-region architecture was meant to eliminate, and this class of hidden dependency is one of the most common reasons a "multi-region" architecture doesn't actually survive a real regional outage in practice.

### 21. How would you decide whether a given service genuinely needs a service mesh, versus the added operational complexity/overhead not being worth it?
A service mesh's value scales with the number of services and the sophistication of traffic management/security requirements between them — for a small number of services (a handful to a dozen), the marginal benefit of mTLS/fine-grained traffic policy/uniform observability often doesn't yet outweigh the real operational cost of running and troubleshooting an additional complex distributed system (sidecar proxy resource overhead per Pod, additional latency per hop, a genuinely non-trivial new failure mode surface, and a real learning curve for the team operating it) — at that scale, simpler alternatives (application-level libraries/mTLS handled at the ingress/load-balancer layer, straightforward NetworkPolicies) frequently deliver most of the needed value with far less operational burden. The calculus shifts as an organization scales to dozens/hundreds of services across multiple teams, where implementing consistent mTLS, retries, and observability *without* a mesh would mean reimplementing (and independently maintaining) that logic in every service/language, and where the mesh's central policy/observability plane becomes a genuine force-multiplier rather than added overhead — the honest senior-level answer explicitly resists "we should adopt a service mesh because it's an industry best practice" as sufficient justification on its own, and instead ties the decision to the organization's actual current scale and pain points.

### 22. Explain how you'd calculate an appropriate SLO and error budget policy for a genuinely novel service with no historical reliability data, and how you'd revisit it over time.
Without historical data, start from the *actual business/user requirement* rather than an arbitrary "everyone uses 99.9%" default — what user-facing behavior would actually be noticed and matter (a batch reporting service failing for 10 minutes might be genuinely unnoticed by users, while a real-time trading/payment path failing for even seconds is highly consequential) — and set an initial SLO deliberately conservative/achievable relative to your genuine current confidence in the system's reliability, explicitly framing it as a starting hypothesis to be revisited with real data, not a permanent commitment set in stone from day one. Instrument the actual SLI faithfully from launch (even before you're confident in the target number, you need real measured data to eventually set an honest one) and revisit the SLO on a regular, planned cadence (quarterly is common) using accumulated real operational data and actual user/business feedback about what reliability level genuinely matters — tightening it if the service is consistently and comfortably beating its target with room to spare (suggesting the target undersells real achieved reliability and a tighter target would better serve users, or conversely that a stricter target is now realistic to promise), or loosening it if the team is chronically burning through error budget while shipping genuinely necessary velocity, prompting an honest conversation about whether the original target was unrealistic for this service's actual risk profile rather than reflexively treating every budget breach as a reliability failure to fix at all costs.
